The Right to Leave

We often describe digital freedom as the right to join an open network. The harder test is whether a person or group can leave a service without losing the records, relationships and authority that made it useful. This issue follows the exit path from a single account to an entire community.

Exit Is a Product Feature

The first time a customer asks how to leave, the answer is usually a download button. That is a start, but a file is only portable if another service can read its structure, preserve its meaning and continue the work. A folder of JSON can be technically complete and practically useless when relationships, permissions and history have been flattened away.

Portability is a product surface, not a legal footer. A useful export has a documented format, a clear map of what it includes, and a receiving path that can accept it without bespoke labour. It also says what cannot move, so the person leaving is not asked to infer absence from silence.

The incentive is easy to see. A system that makes departure costly can mistake friction for loyalty. Make the exit legible and staying becomes a better signal: someone has chosen the service after seeing the alternative.

— Decent Editorial

The Export Is Not the Record

"An export is not a record until someone else can read it."

Article 20 of the GDPR gives a person a defined right to receive personal data they have provided, in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format, and to transmit it to another controller where processing is based on consent or contract and carried out by automated means. It turns a privacy principle into a route for movement.

That route has edges. It does not automatically make every inference, internal score, organisational record or piece of work product portable. Nor does the law promise that a new service will know how to make sense of an exported file. The distinction matters when a service advertises a complete export and a person needs the history behind a number.

A stronger design treats the legal right as a floor. It identifies the record, explains what travels and offers a usable format for what belongs to the person. Data moves more safely when its origin and limits move with it.

— Decent Editorial

The Cloud's Toll Gate Has a Date

Cloud switching has a date on the calendar. The EU Data Act has applied since 12 September 2025. Its transition period allows some switching charges while providers adapt, then the European Commission says switching charges, including data-egress charges, must be removed from 12 January 2027. The provision changes what a supplier can charge, not what a system was built to depend on.

That distinction is useful. Removing a toll can make an alternative financially possible. It cannot make the destination compatible, train a team to operate it, or replace an undocumented dependency. Those are the engineering costs left after the invoice stops.

Cloud contracts now have a clearer exit clause in law. The more interesting work is to build an exit path that already knows its data shapes, its destination and its failure modes. Then the statutory date changes the options on the table instead of starting a rescue project.

— Decent Editorial

Federation Is Not Portability

$ ActivityPub defines both a client-to-server protocol and a server-to-server protocol for federated social networks. That lets separate servers exchange activities while people keep different homes. A message can cross a boundary without putting every account in one database.
$
$ An account migration is a different operation. Moving a handle, posts, followers, blocks, moderation state and delivery history requires rules beyond message exchange. One server can deliver a new post to another and still offer no complete path for a person to move their home.
$
$ That is not a failure of federation. It is a reminder that interoperability is a family of promises. A protocol should name which one it makes, and a product should not imply that one form of connection includes every other form of portability.

A Handle Is a Pointer, Not a Home

A

decentralised identifier is a name resolved by a method, not a magic token that floats free of infrastructure. W3C's DID Core standard describes a data model and operations for decentralised identifiers, while each DID method specifies how a particular identifier is created, resolved, updated or deactivated.

That split matters. An identifier can reduce dependence on a single directory, but a method may still rely on particular operators, registries or recovery paths. Control lives in the whole chain of keys, resolvers and governance, not in the word decentralised printed on the label.

Identity portability asks two questions at once: can a person prove that an identifier is theirs, and can another service interpret the proof without asking the first service for permission? A good system makes the answer inspectable and gives the person a recovery route that survives a lost device.

— Decent Editorial

A Credential Can Travel. Consent Must Follow.

A verifiable credential can carry an issuer's claim in a format that a holder can present to a verifier. The W3C model gives that exchange a shared structure. This is useful when proof needs to move between services without repeating every application from scratch.

Proof is not permission. A credential format cannot decide whether a verifier needs a full record or one attribute, whether a request is fair, or how long a copy should live. Portability that multiplies disclosure is a faster route to exposure, not more agency.

The useful design asks for the smallest claim that answers the question, explains who will see it and makes revocation or replacement part of the system. A credential can travel. Consent has to travel with the moment of presentation.

— Decent Editorial

An Account Needs a Moving Day

The AT Protocol account-migration guide treats moving an account as a planned operation, not a profile setting. It describes creating a new home for the existing identity, copying the public repository, transferring media blobs, moving private preferences where the service supports them, and then updating the identity to point to the new host.

The guide is candid about what may not move. Preferences stored in external services can remain behind, a missing repository backup can mean missing posts, and some migration mechanisms are implementation details that may evolve. A strong migration guide says which steps require the old host, which can be made with a self-controlled key, and what happens when a host is unavailable.

A moving day is only safe when the address, keys and boxes move together. Account portability is a sequence of operations with recovery paths, not a promise hidden in a settings menu.

— Decent Editorial

The Agent's Passport Should Expire

An AI agent that can read a file, send a message or change a record is acting through permissions someone has granted it. The current Model Context Protocol specification defines an authorisation flow for clients accessing restricted servers. That gives a technical shape to a question that every agent interface needs to answer: what may this process do on a person's behalf?

A permission should name the action, the resource and the period for which access is needed. Reading a folder is not the same as publishing from it. A one-time task does not need a standing credential. An agent that keeps the same authority after its job ends turns convenience into an invisible delegation.

The passport metaphor is useful only if it includes borders and expiry. Access should be narrow, visible and revocable. The person remains the principal, and the agent borrows authority for a purpose rather than inheriting it by default.

— Decent Editorial

09

A Community Is More Than a User Table

When a person leaves a service, the export is often framed as an individual matter. A community has a harder problem. Its continuity may depend on who was a member, which decisions were made, what authority each role carried, and which promises still bind the group. A list of names cannot answer those questions.

Portable governance is not a demand to expose every private conversation. It is a way to preserve the evidence that makes shared decisions accountable: the rule in force, the authorised decision, its result and the records needed to contest or revise it. Sensitive material can remain private while the authority to act stays legible.

A group is not portable unless its decisions are legible. The same principle applies to a new home for a collective: take the rulebook and decision history, not just the mailing list, and let the people affected verify what the new system claims to inherit.

— Decent Editorial

The Exit Test

An exit test begins before anyone is packing. Can a person export the data that matters, explain what is missing, and import the rest somewhere else? Can a group carry its membership and decisions without publishing private material? Can an agent's access be revoked without breaking the person's own account?

The European Commission's April 2026 review of the Digital Markets Act offers one early measure of movement. It reports that thousands of end users were using portability tools across designated platforms, and around 40 third parties authorised by users had integrated with gatekeeper APIs. Those figures tell us that a legal right can produce working routes. They do not tell us whether every route is complete or whether people can use it without help.

The best exit is rehearsed before anyone needs it. A service that can show its export, its destination and its recovery plan has made a claim that can be checked. That is a stronger promise than asking people to trust that the door will open later.

— Decent Editorial
